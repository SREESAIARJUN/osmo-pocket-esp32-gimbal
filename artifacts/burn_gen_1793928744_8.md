# STM32H743 AIO Flight Controller with 4-in-1 ESC (Nano Form Factor)
## Power-Stage Loss Model, Thermal Analysis, BDShot600 DMA Plan, and Reference Netlist

## 0. Key Finding: 80 A Burst Is Not Feasible on a Nano Board

I don't have the original Job #37 requirements, so the parameters in §1 are stated assumptions. Replace them with your actual BOM values. The analysis does show a structural problem you should resolve before layout:

> **80 A per channel on a 20×20 mm (or even 25.5×25.5 mm) board is not thermally achievable for more than about 1 s, even under optimistic assumptions.** Commercial 20×20 4-in-1 ESCs are rated about 25–40 A continuous and 35–45 A burst for good reason. The math is in §3.

There are also two architectural constraints worth confirming:

1. **The H743 does not drive the bridges.** In every practical 4-in-1 ESC design, each motor (or the whole 4-in-1) has its own ESC MCU, typically AT32F421 or STM32G071 running AM32/BLHeli_32. These run the commutation loop and 48 kHz PWM. The H743 runs ArduPilot and talks DShot to them. This document assumes that architecture.
2. **System current is 4 × 80 A = 320 A.** No nano-class connector (XT30: about 30 A continuous) or 6S pack lead at this scale supports that. The input capacitor bank and battery pad copper also become dominant design problems.

## 2. Loss Model per Motor Channel

In 6-step synchronous operation, one high-side and one low-side FET carry the phase current at every instant. When the PWM leg is off, its complementary FET carries the current, so the channel always has **two FETs in the conduction path**. Each of the 6 FETs conducts for about one-third of the electrical cycle.

### 2.1 Conduction loss

$$P_{cond} = 2\,I^2\,R_{DS(on)}(T_j)$$

$$P_{cond} = 2 \times 80^2 \times 2.25\,\text{m}\Omega = \mathbf{28.8\ W}$$

Copper loss in the same path:

$$P_{cu} = I^2 R_{cu} = 6400 \times 0.5\,\text{m}\Omega = \mathbf{3.2\ W}$$

### 2.2 Switching (V-I overlap) loss

Only the PWM leg hard-switches:

$$P_{sw} = \tfrac{1}{2} V_{bus}\, I\,(t_r + t_f)\, f_{sw}$$

$$P_{sw} = 0.5 \times 25.2 \times 80 \times 20\,\text{ns} \times 48\,\text{kHz} = \mathbf{0.97\ W}$$

### 2.3 Dead-time body-diode conduction

$$P_{dt} = V_F\, I\, (2\,t_{dt})\, f_{sw} = 0.8 \times 80 \times 400\,\text{ns} \times 48\,\text{kHz} = \mathbf{1.23\ W}$$

At 48 kHz, dead-time loss exceeds switching loss. Tightening dead time is the cheapest efficiency gain, provided shoot-through margin is verified on the scope.

### 2.4 Capacitive and recovery losses

$$P_{oss} = Q_{oss}\,V_{bus}\,f_{sw} = 30\,\text{nC}\times25.2\times48\,\text{k} = 0.036\ \text{W}$$

$$P_{rr} = Q_{rr}\,V_{bus}\,f_{sw} = 20\,\text{nC}\times25.2\times48\,\text{k} = 0.024\ \text{W}$$

### 2.5 Gate drive loss (dissipated mostly in the driver and gate resistors)

$$P_{gate} = 2\,Q_g\,V_{GS}\,f_{sw} = 2\times40\,\text{nC}\times10\times48\,\text{k} = 0.038\ \text{W}$$

### 2.6 Totals

| Loss term | Per motor (W) | 4 motors (W) |
|---|---|---|
| Conduction (FETs) | 28.8 | 115.2 |
| Copper | 3.2 | 12.8 |
| Dead-time diode | 1.23 | 4.9 |
| Switching overlap | 0.97 | 3.9 |
| Q_oss + Q_rr + gate | 0.10 | 0.4 |
| **Total** | **≈34.3** | **≈137** |

**Conduction dominates at about 85% of the total.** Raising f_sw from 24 to 48 kHz costs only about 2.3 W per motor. The real lever is R_DS(on) and copper, and the 3×3 package caps how low R_DS(on) can go.

The general form, useful for sweeping parameters, is:

$$P_{ch}(I,T_j)=2I^2R_{25}\big[1+\alpha(T_j-25)\big]+I^2R_{cu}+\tfrac12V I(t_r+t_f)f_{sw}+2V_F I t_{dt} f_{sw}+(Q_{oss}+Q_{rr})Vf_{sw}+2Q_gV_{GS}f_{sw}$$

Here α ≈ 0.005 /K reproduces the 1.5× factor at 125 °C. Because R_DS(on) rises with temperature and conduction loss rises with R_DS(on), the system has positive electrothermal feedback. Iterate P → T_j → R → P until it converges or runs away.

## 3. Thermal Analysis

### 3.1 Per-FET instantaneous dissipation

The conducting FET carries 80 A continuously during its one-third interval:

$$P_{FET,inst} = I^2 R_{hot} = 6400 \times 2.25\,\text{m}\Omega = 14.4\ \text{W}$$

The average per FET is about 34.3 W / 6 ≈ 5.7 W. At eRPM-scale commutation rates, the die's thermal time constant (milliseconds) is much longer than one electrical cycle, so the average is the relevant figure.
