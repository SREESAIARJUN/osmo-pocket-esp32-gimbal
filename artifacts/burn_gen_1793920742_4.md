## 0. Feasibility Flags

These issues affect everything downstream and should be resolved first.

1. **80 A per channel on a "nano" (20×20 mm) board is not thermally viable as a sustained rating.** The model below shows roughly 100 W of power-stage dissipation at 80 A×4. That makes 80 A a **sub-2-second burst rating** only, and only with prop-wash airflow. Choose one:
   - Keep 20×20 mm and derate to about 35 A continuous / 55 A burst, or
   - Move to 30.5×30.5 mm with 3×3 mm or 5×6 mm PQFN FETs.
2. **The H743 does not commutate the motors.** In every viable architecture, each ESC channel (or one 4-channel ESC MCU) runs AM32 or BLHeli_32 on its own MCU, such as the AT32F421 or STM32G071. The H743 runs ArduPilot and *sends* DShot. The "48 kHz switching" figure is an ESC-firmware parameter, not an H743 function.
3. **Placeholder parameters.** All component parameters below are representative values. Replace them with datasheet values for the parts you actually select, then re-run the model. The equations are general; the numbers are illustrative.

## 1. Power-Stage Topology and Assumptions

Each channel is a 3-phase bridge (6 N-FETs) with a 3-phase gate driver (for example, FD6288Q class), driven by synchronous 6-step trapezoidal commutation with complementary PWM.

## 2. Conduction-Loss Model

### 2.1 Current path in 6-step complementary PWM

During any commutation sector, two phases are active:

- **Phase X (PWM leg):**
  - The high-side FET conducts for a fraction D of each period.
  - The low-side FET (synchronous rectifier) conducts for 1 − D.
- **Phase Y (return leg):** the low-side FET is continuously on.

So, per channel, the conduction loss is:

$$
P_{cond}=I_{rms}^2 R_{ds}(T_j)\,[\,D+(1-D)\,]+I_{rms}^2R_{ds}(T_j)=2\,I_{rms}^2R_{ds}(T_j)
$$

This is independent of duty cycle, which is a useful result: worst-case conduction loss occurs at any throttle where I = 80 A.

**Ripple correction.** For a phase current with peak-to-peak ripple ΔI:

$$
I_{rms}^2 = I_{avg}^2 + \frac{\Delta I^2}{12}
$$

Motor inductance at 48 kHz typically keeps this term below 2%, so it is neglected here.

### 2.2 Temperature-dependent R_ds(on) and the closed-form junction temperature

$$
R_{ds}(T_j)=R_{ds,25}\,[1+\alpha(T_j-25)]
$$

Per FET (k_d is the fraction of time that FET conducts; for the always-on return FET, k_d = 1):

$$
P_{fet}=k_d\, I^2R_{ds}(T_j),\qquad T_j=T_{ref}+P_{fet}\,R_{\theta}
$$

Substituting and solving for T_j:

$$
\boxed{T_j=\frac{T_{ref}+k_d I^2R_{ds,25}R_\theta(1-25\alpha)}{1-k_d I^2R_{ds,25}\,\alpha\,R_\theta}}
$$

**Thermal-runaway criterion:**

$$
k_d\, I^2 R_{ds,25}\,\alpha\,R_\theta \ge 1
$$

At I = 80 A and k_d = 1, runaway occurs when:

$$
R_\theta \ge \frac{1}{6400 \times 1.2\times10^{-3} \times 0.0045} \approx 29\ \text{K/W}
$$

This is the effective junction-to-ambient resistance of a single FET. On a small, unheatsunk board, local R_θ,ja for a 3×3 mm PQFN can approach 30–50 K/W. **This is the formal reason 80 A continuous on a nano board is impossible.**

## 3. Switching-Loss Model at 48 kHz

Only the PWM leg switches. Per channel:

$$
P_{sw}=\underbrace{\tfrac12 V I (t_r+t_f) f_{sw}}_{\text{V–I overlap}}+\underbrace{Q_{rr}V f_{sw}}_{\text{reverse recovery}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12 Q_{oss}V f_{sw}}_{\text{C}_{oss}}+\underbrace{2V_fI\,t_{dt} f_{sw}}_{\text{dead-time diode}}
$$

| Term | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Overlap | 0.5 × 25.2 × 80 × 20 ns × 48 k | 0.968 W |
| Reverse recovery | 40 nC × 25.2 × 48 k | 0.048 W |
| C_oss | 0.5 × 30 nC × 25.2 × 48 k | 0.018 W |
| Dead time | 2 × 0.8 × 80 × 50 ns × 48 k | 0.307 W |
| **Switching per channel** | | **≈ 1.34 W** |

**Gate-drive power**, mostly dissipated in the driver and gate resistors:

$$
P_g = Q_g V_{GS} f_{sw} \times 2 \text{ FETs} = 40\text{n}\times10\times48\text{k}\times2 \approx 0.04\ \text{W}
$$

### Design insights

- At 80 A, conduction loss is about **95% of the total**. Raising f_sw from 24 kHz to 48 kHz costs only about 0.7 W per channel but gives smoother, quieter low-throttle control.
- Dead time is the largest switching sub-term. In AM32, reducing dead time from 50 ns to 30 ns saves about 0.12 W per channel, but you must verify shoot-through margin on a scope.
